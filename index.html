Your targets

Pacing

  • Bike: 70.3 racing sits between your two thresholds, near the top of that band for a race this length. Hold race power, allow the top of race intensity on climbs, never threshold. The first 15 minutes stay below race power while your heart rate settles.
  • Run: first 3 km at the slow end of race pace even though it'll feel easy. Hold race pace to 16 km, then race whatever is left.
  • The classic error: racing harder than you've trained. Every key session from June rehearses the exact intensity you'll race at, so the race should feel familiar, not heroic.

Fuelling

  • Breakfast you've tested, about 3 hours before: 100–150 g carbohydrate, low fibre.
  • Bike: 60–90 g carbohydrate and 500–750 ml fluid per hour, starting 10 minutes in. You'll have practised this on every long ride since October.
  • Run: a gel or cola at aid stations from 5 km, aiming for 40–60 g per hour.
  • Train with the drink and gels you'll race with from June onwards. Check the athlete guide for the on-course brand.

The course

  • Single-lap sea swim in Weymouth Bay, with a self-seeded rolling start. Seed yourself honestly by goal time.
  • One-lap bike into the rolling Dorset countryside. Reported climbing varies from about 800 m to about 1,000 m: rolling rather than mountainous, with long open stretches where clip-on aero bars pay off.
  • Flat run along the seafront in laps. The lap count has changed between years, so check the athlete guide and count laps from your watch.
  • Week 41 has a course recce ride built in. Weymouth is under two hours from Bristol.

Taper

Week 46 drops to about 75–80% of peak volume, week 47 to about 55%, race week to about a third. Intensity stays in every week: race-pace work keeps you sharp, the reduced volume lets fatigue clear. Lifting drops to light sessions, then mobility only in race week.

The August Olympic-distance race

If you confirm a London Olympic-distance race, the best home for it is week 43 (2–8 August), which is already a recovery week, so it becomes the mini-taper. Late July works too. Avoid anything after about 22 August: too close to Weymouth.

It gets treated as a real race, not a training day: two or three lighter days before, three or four easy days after. Send me the date when you have it and I'll rework the weeks around it.

Kit the plan assumes

  • A smart trainer or power meter. Most winter bike sessions are written for indoors, and power is how the threshold work gets controlled. Heart rate and effort work as a fallback.
  • Clip-on aero bars and a basic bike fit on the road bike, ideally before February.
  • A wetsuit by May for open water, and a tow float.
  • A chest heart-rate strap, and optionally an HRV app or watch for the morning readiness check.

Method and sources

Four coaching philosophies, one plan. Where they agree (lots of easy volume, consistency, controlled intensity, slow progression) the plan follows all of them. Where they differ, it picks by phase.

Starting point the plan is built for

  • A runner doing 30–50 km a week, coming off a half marathon on 11 October 2026.
  • Not yet able to swim 1.5 km freestyle continuously, so the swim gets the most technical attention early.
  • Owns a road bike but rarely rides, so bike volume ramps from low and never jumps.
  • 14 hours a week of swim, bike and run as a ceiling, not a starting point. Lifting sits on top.
  • Swims every Monday, Wednesday and Friday. Lifting slots are reserved on the same days, straight after the swim, so it's one trip.

Dan Lorang

Coach to Jan Frodeno, Anne Haug and Lucy Charles-Barclay; head of performance at Red Bull–Bora–Hansgrohe.

What he does

  • Periodises in repeating phases: technique and VO2max first, then strength and endurance, then "economisation" with race-pace intervals close to competition. The cycle repeats two or three times a year.
  • Run: hill reps for VO2max; long uphill runs at low intensity for strength (he uses 4–5 × 2 km); long runs alternating 1 km at race pace with 1 km easy late in the build.
  • Treats VO2max work as a controlled intensity, not sprinting, and keeps the rest of training easy.
  • Tests inside training with repeatable sessions such as 8 × 1 km rather than stopping to test.
  • Slow, healthy progression. Frodeno credits him with slowing him down, and the consistency that followed.
  • Races are never training sessions.

Where it shows up

  • The whole phase structure: phases 1–2, then the cycle again in phases 3–5.
  • Hill reps on Thursdays in phase 1; long uphill reps in phases 2 and 4.
  • Alternating race-pace kilometres in the phase 5 long runs.
  • The 8 × 1 km benchmark in every test week.
  • The August race, if it happens, gets a real taper.

Dr Dan Plews

Sports scientist (PhD in heart-rate variability), coach, Kona age-group course record holder and the fastest age-group Ironman on record.

What he does

  • Points out that Ironman and 70.3 racing happen mostly at moderate intensity, between the two thresholds, and 70.3 sits near the top of that band.
  • So the intensity mix should span polarised (roughly 80/20) early in the season and pyramidal (more moderate work) as race day nears.
  • Uses low-cadence (60–75 rpm), moderate-intensity bike intervals for threshold power.
  • Monitors readiness with HRV; has published on heat training and intensity distribution.
  • Does much of his own training indoors for time efficiency.

Where it shows up

  • The target intensity mix by phase (table below).
  • Low-cadence intervals in phase 2, at race power in phase 4.
  • The readiness rule below.
  • Indoor-friendly winter sessions.

Lance Watson

Founder of LifeSport Coaching; has coached Olympic and Ironman champions, and specialises in time-limited age-groupers.

What he does

  • Treats November to January as a "shoulder season" for skills (including single-leg pedalling drills at 70–90 rpm) before base training proper.
  • Uses three kinds of 70.3 brick: bike-emphasis, run-emphasis and race simulation. His half-ironman bricks combine 20-minute race-intensity blocks, 6 × 3 min at 60–70 rpm late in the ride, then a run at race pace.
  • In the final 6–8 weeks: runs off the bike at goal pace (for example 30 min at goal pace plus 15 easy) and 4 × 1 mile 10–15 s faster than goal pace; race fuelling dialled in.
  • Warns that the most common 70.3 error is racing harder than you trained.

Where it shows up

  • Pedalling skills and swim technique focus in phase 1.
  • Tuesday runs off the bike from phase 2 onwards.
  • Watson bricks A and B on phase 5 Saturdays, the race simulation and the dress rehearsal.
  • Thursday mile repeats in phase 5; fuelling practice on every long ride.

Olav Aleksander Bu and Kristian Blummenfelt

The Norwegian method: Olympic, Ironman and 70.3 world titles.

What they do

  • High volume at low intensity, plus a lot of work between LT1 and LT2, which makes the model pyramidal rather than polarised. Intensity is controlled tightly, originally by blood lactate.
  • Double-threshold days at the elite end; 25–35 hour weeks for Blummenfelt.
  • Blummenfelt's running, as analysed by Alan Couzens: mostly short, frequent easy runs with a key session every few days, including tempo rolling into threshold inside a long run.
  • A heat block early in the season, then a few heat sessions a week to maintain it. Blummenfelt says it helps even in cool races.
  • Bu himself has said they could drop much of their technology and still get to the same place.

Where it shows up

  • Phase 3 sub-threshold intervals on bike and run, with power and heart-rate ceilings standing in for lactate.
  • Frequent easy runs with strides and hill sprints.
  • Tempo-to-threshold blocks inside two phase 3 long runs.
  • The heat block in weeks 37–38 and maintenance to week 46.

What's deliberately left out

  • Double-threshold days. The principle underneath them is accumulating more controlled threshold time. At 14 hours with a young bike engine, spreading it across the week does that with less risk.
  • Lactate testing. Power, pace and heart-rate ceilings do the same job well enough for an age-grouper.
  • Plews' low-carbohydrate race fuelling. He's an advocate, but for a first 70.3 at this duration, a standard high-carbohydrate plan that you've practised is the safer bet.

The readiness rule

Each morning, take your HRV (watch or app) and note how you feel. If your 7-day average sits below your normal range for two days or more and you feel flat, swap that day's key session for easy endurance at the same duration. Don't try to make it up later. Missed sessions are cheap; digging a hole in week 30 is expensive.
